# Six Personas — impact.com

Three partners, three brands. Built from 267 interviews, 158 G2 reviews and 90 days of product behaviour.

**Structure:** Cooper goal-directed archetypes with a jobs-to-be-done layer inside each — the job statement, its functional / emotional / social dimensions, and how that job differs between a creator and an affiliate.

**Names** are drawn from the US and UK, the two largest geographies in the partner base (United States 498,473 users; United Kingdom 57,516) and the base all research recruited from. No age, location or pronoun is assigned, because none of those were findings — the behaviour was.

**Original structure note:** Cooper goal-directed archetypes. Each persona carries a goal hierarchy (life / experience / end goals), the job they hired us for, frustrations, behaviours, a day-in-the-life scenario and design implications. One primary persona per side.

**Live version** — charts wired to live PostHog and Outset, exportable persona cards, and a persona you can question directly: https://claude.ai/artifact/FaBjbFu7hiM5LYBfALemAQ

# Partner side

**For a partner, impact.com is a waypoint.** The content, the audience and the earning all happen elsewhere, so every second spent here is overhead on someone else's work. Speed is the whole design problem on this side.

Grounded in 105 Partner Exp interviews, 119 Mobile App Exp interviews, 12 contextual inquiries and 90 days of behaviour. Every percentage carries its real denominator.

## P1 — Nia Bennett, the New Signup

**Biggest unmet need —** To know an application was even seen

**Confidence 64/100 (Moderate) · 3 data sources.** 26 interviews at this tier · contextual inquiry · full signup telemetry

*Known gap:* No partner who signed up and never activated has ever been interviewed.


> "I'm supposed to have an affiliate code. I can't find it… I'm lost."
> — P5, Getting Started, Outset Partner Exp

### Goals
- **Life goal** — To make creating a living rather than a hobby she apologises for.
- **Experience goal** — To stop feeling like everyone else got a manual she didn't.
- **End goals** — Get approved by one brand she'd genuinely recommend. See one real sale. Find out whether she is doing this right.

### The job she hired us for
> When I've signed up because this is supposedly how creators get paid, I want to reach the point where I have a working link and a brand that said yes, so I can find out whether any of this is real for someone like me.

### Job dimensions
- **Functional** — Find a brand that will say yes, get its link, and confirm a sale actually happened.
- **Emotional** — Relief at not being lost. Proof this is real before the motivation runs out.
- **Social** — To be seen by brands as worth a recurring relationship, not a one-off.

> **How this job differs for an affiliate.** For a creator, every application is also a judgement about whether the brand suits the audience, so a rejection reads as personal. An affiliate at this stage would be optimising for programme count and payout terms rather than fit — but no affiliate at this stage has ever been interviewed, so that sentence is a hypothesis, not a finding.

### Frustrations
- Cannot locate the basics — the affiliate code, the assets page, the link.
- Doesn't know what the platform can do, and isn't told. *"I don't even know that these are here. Because how would I know? I'm not notified."*
- Applies and hears nothing back. No status, no reason, no timeline.
- No benchmark. She cannot tell whether $40 in a month is good, bad or normal.

### The data — where she sits in the activation funnel
Unique partner users, 90 days to 8 Oct 2026:

| Step | Users | Share of signup starts |
|---|---:|---:|
| Signup start screen seen | 894,562 | 100% |
| Email submitted | 277,920 | 31.1% |
| Signup completed | 248,006 | 27.7% |
| Business model submitted | 245,160 | 27.4% |
| **Partner profile submitted** | **144,822** | **16.2%** |
| Link generated in PublisherPro | 15,124 | 1.7% |
| **Tracking link copied** | **8,272** | **0.9%** |

A 41% drop between stating a business model and finishing a profile. Two surfaces generate links, so true reach sits between 15,124 and 23,396 — either way the core partner job is completed by a small fraction of those who sign up.

### Creator and affiliate: where this job diverges

**Creator — evidenced.** Applications are judged on audience fit, so a rejection reads as a verdict on who follows them. They apply to a handful of brands they would genuinely recommend.

**Affiliate — hypothesis.** Would apply at volume across categories, filtering on payout and cost model. Silence would be an operational cost rather than a personal one.

**What it changes for us.** Closing the loop for Nia needs a **reason**. For an affiliate at the same stage it needs a **queue**. Same unmet need, two different products.

### A day in the life
Nia checks her phone before work. There's a notification: *"You're pre-approved, start earning now."* She taps it, lands on a list of eligible brands, and applies to four. Six minutes, and it feels like progress.

At lunch she wants to add a link to a post she's drafting. She finds the brand she was approved for last week but can't work out where the link lives — the page sends her to a desktop view that doesn't fit her screen. She screenshots it to deal with later and posts without the link.

That evening she opens the Excel sheet she's kept since January and writes in the two commissions that landed. Of the four applications she sent this morning, none will ever be answered. She'll apply again tomorrow.

### The workflow, stage by stage

How the work actually goes, where it breaks, and what we do about it.

| Stage | What they do | Friction |
|---|---|---|
| **Sign up** | Completes signup on a phone in a few minutes. | Smooth |
| **Build the profile** | Writes a real bio — 121,539 partners write 250+ characters. | High |
| **Find brands** | Checks notifications, opens the pre-approved list, browses eligible brands. | Some |
| **Apply and wait** | Applies to several brands in one sitting. | Blocked |
| **Get the link** | Goes to find the tracking link for a brand they were approved for. | Blocked |
| **Publish** | Puts the link into a post or a video description. | Smooth |
| **Check earnings** | Opens the spreadsheet kept in parallel since January. | High |


## P2 — Danny Alvarez, the Working Partner

**Biggest unmet need —** A reliable signal of likely return, before the effort is spent

**Confidence 81/100 (High) · 4 data sources.** 105 + 119 interviews · contextual inquiry · device and link telemetry

*Known gap:* No affiliate-type partner has been interviewed. This is a creator's view of the job.


> "The part I worry most, it's the time… I don't want to spend the time on something that's not paying back."
> — B-0051, Scaling Up, Contextual Inquiry 2026/27


### Goals
- **Life goal** — Income that doesn't depend on one platform's algorithm deciding he still matters.
- **Experience goal** — To feel the platform respects his time rather than consuming it.
- **End goals** — Pick offers that pay back. Place links without breaking his production rhythm. See what each one earned.

### The job he hired us for
> When I'm producing on a schedule and partnerships are real income, I want to know which offers are worth my effort and place their links without breaking my rhythm, so I can make this predictable enough to rely on.

### Job dimensions
- **Functional** — Judge an offer's return before committing, generate links fast, see what each one earned.
- **Emotional** — Confidence the time will pay back. The sharpest anxiety here is wasted effort, not difficulty.
- **Social** — To read as an authentic curator rather than as someone selling.

> **How this job differs for an affiliate.** *The audience is the asset.* A bad promotion costs trust that took years to build, so Danny under-promotes rather than risk it — which makes "will this offer pay back?" partly a trust question, not just an ROI one. An affiliate has no audience to protect and would simply run the offer and measure it, which is why bulk generation and feed syndication dominate their requests and barely register here. Untested: Partner Exp excluded affiliates by screener.

### Frustrations
- **He commits before he knows.** Nothing tells him whether an offer converts until he's already spent the week on it.
- Advanced tasks collapse on mobile — deep links, vanity links and asset downloads all bounce him to desktop.
- The platform has stalled on him before and he remembers, though he now says it's noticeably faster.
- Payout timing and fee deductions are hard to predict, which makes planning harder than earning.

### The data — what he needs before he commits
Share of 105 Partner Exp interviewees naming each, full-sample denominators:

| Need | Share |
|---|---:|
| Precise product and deal detail | 41% (43 of 105) |
| Clear commission terms | 30% (31 of 105) |
| Performance signal — EPC, conversion rate | 28% (29 of 105) |
| **Leaves for own tools when it stalls** | **23% (24 of 105)** |

### The data — mobile is the bigger doorway, the smaller workshop
Partner-side events, 90 days:

| Device | People reached | Share of activity |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mobile | 584,980 | 19.3% |
| Desktop | 540,375 | 79.9% |

More unique partners touch the platform on a phone than on a desktop, but a desktop session carries about four and a half times more work. Feature parity is the wrong goal; task-appropriate parity is the right one.

### Creator and affiliate: where this job diverges

**Creator — evidenced.** "Will this pay back?" is partly a trust question — a bad promotion costs credibility built over years, so they under-promote rather than risk it. Six links per piece of content, by hand, often on a phone.

**Affiliate — hypothesis.** No audience to protect, so the same question is purely return on effort. Would generate links in bulk and syndicate through feeds or an API; one-at-a-time flows would not scale.

**What it changes for us.** Expected EPC serves both. But Danny also needs **brand-fit signal**, and an affiliate needs **throughput**. Ship the number for both; ship the bulk tooling for one.

### A day in the life
Danny films in the morning. By eleven he has a tutorial that mentions six products and needs six links before he can finish the edit. He opens the laptop, goes to reports first out of habit — yesterday had plenty of clicks and no conversions, which bothers him more than a quiet day would.

He works through the brands one at a time. Two are straightforward. One has had its assets moved since the redesign and takes four extra clicks. One he isn't approved for yet, applied three weeks ago, and has heard nothing about. He drops that product from the script rather than wait.

At four he's out with a friend when a follower DMs asking where to buy something from last week's video. He knows the link exists. He cannot get to it from his phone. He says he'll send it later, and by the evening he has forgotten.

### The workflow, stage by stage

How the work actually goes, where it breaks, and what we do about it.

| Stage | What they do | Friction |
|---|---|---|
| **Review performance** | Opens reports first out of habit — overview, revenue by day, clicks by site. | Smooth |
| **Choose offers** | Decides which products are worth building content around. | Blocked |
| **Generate links** | Six links, brand by brand, before the edit can finish. | Some |
| **Publish** | Drops the links into the video description. | Smooth |
| **Answer the audience** | A follower DMs asking where to buy something from last week. | Blocked |
| **Track what converted** | Checks clicks against sales. | Some |
| **Get paid** | Waits for the payout to land. | High |


## P3 — Priya Shah, the Operator

**Biggest unmet need —** Sub-ID-level data out of the platform, without rebuilding it elsewhere

**Confidence 58/100 (Moderate) · 3 data sources.** 25 interviews · approval telemetry · region telemetry

*Known gap:* No publisher network or deal-site operator interviewed, despite being closest to this tier.


> "I like to look at the reports. The reports excite me… what sub IDs are working, what sources, what landing pages."
> — P40, Strategic Growth, Outset Partner Exp

### Goals
- **Life goal** — To run this as a business with staff and a succession plan, not a personal income stream.
- **Experience goal** — To be treated as a partner in the commercial sense, not a ticket in a queue.
- **End goals** — Get data out at the grain she thinks in. Automate everything repeatable. Spend effort only where return is already proven.

### The job she hired us for
> When I'm running fifteen-plus programmes as a business with staff, I want to pull data at my own grain and automate everything repeatable, so I can put effort only where the return is already proven.

### Job dimensions
- **Functional** — Pull data at their own grain, automate the repeatable, get decisions faster.
- **Emotional** — To be treated as a commercial partner rather than a ticket in a queue.
- **Social** — To be recognised as a top-tier operator brands seek out rather than screen.

> **Where the creator and affiliate jobs converge.** Priya already thinks like an affiliate — testing creatives, landing pages and geographies, optimising for return rather than fit. This is the tier where the creator / affiliate distinction starts to dissolve, and the one place the current research comes close to the affiliate job without ever having recruited one.

### Frustrations
- **Capability removed without a replacement path.** The campaign cost-model filter — per lead, per sale, per impression — was taken out. Raised twice, unprompted, by the most sophisticated partner in the study.
- Analytics stop short of her grain. She needs sub ID, source and landing page, in her own BI tool.
- Applications sit unanswered for months, capping what she can plan around.
- No relationship management. At her volume, a support queue is the wrong instrument.

### The data — what the approval surface actually records
Brand decisions on partner applications, 180 days:

| Decision | Events | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rejected | 1,376 | 66% |
| Accepted | 691 | 34% |

**Priya's experience of rejection is real; the often-quoted 90% rate is not.** One interviewee described being rejected on almost 90% of his own applications, and that figure then travelled as a platform statistic. What is measurable is roughly two rejections per accept — and application submissions are so thinly instrumented that no true rate can be computed at all.

### The data — where partners like her actually trade
Unique partner users by region, 90 days:

| Region | Users | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| North America | 599,087 | 52.0% |
| Europe | 255,109 | 22.2% |
| Asia | 186,979 | 16.2% |
| Africa | 51,281 | 4.5% |
| South America | 37,446 | 3.3% |
| Oceania | 21,530 | 1.9% |

Just under half of partner activity originates outside North America, and every study so far recruited English speakers. Africa alone is larger than South America and has never been represented in a persona.

### Creator and affiliate: where this job diverges

**Creator — evidenced.** Already optimising the way an affiliate would — testing creatives, landing pages and geographies, and measuring at sub-ID level rather than by brand.

**Affiliate — hypothesis.** Essentially the same job. This is the tier where the distinction dissolves, which is why the removed cost-model filter reads as an affiliate-shaped loss even though a creator raised it.

**What it changes for us.** Very little, and that is the finding. **At this tier, build for the behaviour rather than the type** — the two converge.

### A day in the life
Priya starts in the reports, not the marketplace, looking for one thing: which sub IDs moved yesterday and which landing pages they came from. She exports it, because the view she wants doesn't exist in the product and she rebuilt it in her own dashboard eighteen months ago.

Her analyst asks about a campaign type filter that used to exist — cost per lead versus cost per sale. It was removed in the redesign. There's no replacement, so they filter by hand against a list they maintain themselves.

Mid-morning she reviews the applications her team sent last month. Of forty, eleven came back. Three were approved. The rest are still open, with no status and no date. She stops counting them as pipeline.

### The workflow, stage by stage

How the work actually goes, where it breaks, and what we do about it.

| Stage | What they do | Friction |
|---|---|---|
| **Pull the morning report** | Goes straight to reports, not the marketplace. | Smooth |
| **Slice to sub-ID** | Looks for which sub IDs moved and which landing pages they came from. | Blocked |
| **Filter offers** | Wants to see only cost-per-lead or cost-per-sale campaigns. | Blocked |
| **Apply at volume** | Sends applications across many programmes with a team of two. | High |
| **Chase decisions** | Reviews last month's forty applications. | Blocked |
| **Optimise** | Tests creatives, landing pages and geographies against each other. | Some |
| **Escalate** | Raises something with support. | High |


# Brand side

**For a brand, impact.com is a workspace.** It is where the programme is built, run, measured and paid — so where partners need speed, brands need depth and control. Almost every brand friction point below is depth or control being withheld.

**Three roles, not three company sizes.** The ladder is *how much of the job is yours*: done alongside other work, done as the work, or owned but delegated.

Grounded in 37 Gong sales calls, 158 G2 reviews (Jan 2025 – Jul 2026), 43 Brand Exp interviews and PostHog brand cohorts (~13,700 average brand DAU).

### The brand loop

Brands do not pass through — they stay inside a loop: **Set up → Recruit → Contract → Activate → Measure → Pay → Protect.** Each persona's workflow table below walks that loop at their scale.

**Primary job, all tiers:** run a partnership channel that demonstrably pays back, without the admin burden scaling with it. The automation core delivers this — roughly five hours a week returned — and it is the most consistent reason brands stay.

### Brands are more NAM-concentrated than partners

| Region | Brand DAU | Partner DAU | Partners per brand |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| NAM | ~8,200 (60%) | ~72,400 (49%) | 8.8 |
| EMEA | ~2,700 (20%) | ~36,200 (24%) | 13.4 |
| APAC | ~2,700 (20%) | ~30,100 (20%) | 11.1 |
| LATAM | ~215 (1.6%) | ~7,700 (5%) | 35.8 |

A LATAM partner competes for attention in a market with roughly four times fewer brands per partner than a NAM partner does. The loudest partner complaint — *there aren't enough brands* — is not only a discovery problem. In some regions it is a supply problem, visible in our own DAU data.


## B1 — Sophie Carter · The Founder-Operator

**Biggest unmet need —** Proof the channel pays back before the twelve-month commitment does

**Confidence 62/100 (Moderate) · 3 data sources.** 10 Gong sales calls · 58 G2 reviews · PostHog Lightly Active cohort (~9,700 avg DAU, the largest brand tier)

*Known gap:* No brand in its first 90 days has ever been interviewed.


> "It's complicated, overwhelming, and lacks the support to execute with a small staff."
> — G2 reviewer, Small Business, 2 stars, May 2025

### Goals
- **Life goal** — To build a brand that grows without her personally selling every unit.
- **Experience goal** — To not feel out of her depth in a tool she is paying for.
- **End goals** — One good partner live within a fortnight. Attributed revenue before the quarter closes. Payments she doesn't process by hand.

### The job she hired us for
> When I've committed budget to a channel I've never run before, I want to get a decent partner live and see revenue inside a few weeks, so I can prove this works before anyone asks me to justify the line item.

### Job dimensions
- **Functional** — Get one partner live, see attributed revenue, stop processing payments by hand.
- **Emotional** — To not feel out of their depth in a tool they are already paying for.
- **Social** — To be the founder who proved a new channel works on a small budget.

> **Which partner type they get, versus the one they want.** Sophie is applied to overwhelmingly by *affiliates* — volume applicants whose claimed reach can't be verified — while hoping for *creators* who suit the brand. The mismatch between who applies and who is wanted **is** the problem: nineteen applications, none recognisable, and no signal to tell one type from the other.

### Frustrations
- **It assumes a headcount she doesn't have.** *"Not built for smaller teams, needs a full time resource to manage and maintain the platform."*
- Price scales against her. *"Great product for scale, but expensive for small volume."* All five small-business pricing reviews are negative.
- Her inbound applicants are mostly junk. *"A lot of spam users… people that apply to your program that clearly aren't going to drive traffic or will hurt your brand."*
- She can't verify what applicants claim. *"What other affiliates listed on their profile is not true, especially when it comes to the size of their networks."*

### Worth noticing
Sophie's spam problem is the other half of Nia's silence. She rejects because she is drowning in applicants she can't verify; Nia never hears back because Sophie has no way to triage at speed. Partner vetting and fraud detection is the second-highest feature request in the entire review set.

### A day in the life
Sophie opens the platform roughly twice a week, between a supplier call and a packaging decision. There are nineteen new applications. She recognises none of them. Four list follower counts she suspects are inflated and has no way to check.

She approves two that look legitimate, rejects six that are obviously not, and leaves eleven because deciding properly would take an afternoon she doesn't have. They will still be there next week.

Her co-founder asks what the channel returned last month. Sophie knows the answer is in here somewhere. She exports to a spreadsheet, because that is the format the question will be asked in again.

## B3 — James Whitfield · The Partnerships Lead

**Biggest unmet need —** One global view, and an integration that does not delay launch

**Confidence 58/100 (Moderate) · 4 data sources.** 16 Gong sales calls (the most of any tier) · 21 G2 reviews · interviews · PostHog Power cohort (~780 avg DAU)

*Known gap:* The thinnest base in the set — key findings rest on two or three people each.


> "The UX is really not that good, and I've spent hours explaining to my partners how to deal with things on Impact."
> — G2 reviewer, Enterprise, 1.5 stars, France

### Goals
- **Life goal** — To have partnerships taken as seriously in the business as paid media is.
- **Experience goal** — To be told what is changing before his team finds out from a partner.
- **End goals** — Consolidate onto one system. Get data into the warehouse. Prove the channel at board level.

### The job he hired us for
> When partnerships is a material share of company revenue and I own the number, I want to get the data out in the shape finance already thinks in, so I can defend a multi-year commitment with evidence rather than advocacy.

### Job dimensions
- **Functional** — Consolidate onto one system, get data into the warehouse, prove the channel at board level.
- **Emotional** — Not to be surprised in front of their own team.
- **Social** — To have partnerships taken as seriously internally as paid media already is.

> **Managing every partner type at once.** James runs creators, affiliates and publisher networks simultaneously across three regions, and absorbs the partner-side UX cost of all three — which is exactly what the one-and-a-half-star enterprise review describes. A partner-side improvement for Danny shows up on James's retention, not on Danny's.

### Frustrations
- **He absorbs the platform's UX cost on behalf of his partners.** Three of four enterprise interface reviews are negative, and the complaint is about explaining it to others.
- Unannounced changes land as shock. *"I logged in. I thought something happened, and then started slowly seeing other people's dashboards were changing."*
- The API works but throttles. *"The data pull limits are rather low."*
- Reports strain under his own complexity. *"It sometimes struggles with the complexity of my multi-brand, multi-programme setup."*

### A day in the life
James logs in on Monday and the dashboard has moved. Nobody told him. His first thought is that something is broken, and his second is that three of his regional managers are about to ask him the same question.

He spends twenty minutes writing an internal note explaining the change he has just discovered, because he'd rather his team hear it from him.

In the afternoon he's pulling a quarterly view for the CMO. The report is genuinely good — granular, accurate, customisable. It just takes two attempts to load at his data volume, and the API limit means his analyst can't simply schedule it into the warehouse instead.

## Sources and how to read the numbers

**Sources.** Outset: Partner Exp (105 interviews, 13 studies), Mobile App Exp (119 interviews), Brand Exp (43 interviews, 7 studies), Contextual Inquiry 2026/27 (n=12). G2: 158 brand and advertiser reviews, Jan 2025 – Jul 2026 (65 mid-market, 58 small business, 21 enterprise). PostHog: 90- and 180-day behavioural pulls taken 8 October 2026.

**Reading the numbers.** Every percentage states its denominator. Behavioural figures are one-off aggregates — no governed metric definitions exist for them. Device and region counts exclude PublisherPro, which is not browser-attributed. Named quotes come from research participants and public G2 reviews; the personas themselves are composites and are not real individuals.

**Known gaps.**
1. No partner who signed up and never activated has been interviewed — every screener requires active use, and that group is the largest by an order of magnitude.
2. Deal, coupon, review, email and network partners were excluded by the Partner Exp screener, and they carry the highest median commissions.
3. No study has recruited outside English, though just under half of partner activity is outside North America and Africa alone is 51,281 users.
4. Application submission is effectively uninstrumented, so time-to-decision cannot be measured on either side.

## The two-sided marketplace finding

The sharpest evidence in the whole corpus is a **brand** criticising our **partner** rejection email in a public review:

> "It provides no actionable detail on why a partner was rejected." — Process Pioneers, LLC

A partner and a brand, in two unrelated studies, describing the same broken artefact from opposite ends.

Set that against the numbers: **42.5% of 1.86M applications declined**, and marketplace-originated partnerships converting at **3.6–4.9%** against **17.6–23.1%** for a branded signup. These are not two problems. One marketplace is failing in both directions at once — brands cannot find partners worth approving, partners cannot find brands that will approve them, and the resulting relationships convert at a fifth of the rate of the direct path.

**Discovery and vetting are the same product problem.** A brand's job is *find partners worth having*. Today we optimise for reach into a large pool and leave quality assessment entirely to the brand — so their workload scales with the size of the pool we hand them. That is why "more partners" does not read as a benefit.
